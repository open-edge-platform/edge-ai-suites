Mahabharata: Chapter on the Game of Dice and Exile

In the royal hall of Hastinapura, the air was heavy with pride, jealousy, and destiny. Duryodhana, unable to bear the prosperity and honor of the Pandavas, persuaded his uncle Shakuni to challenge Yudhishthira to a game of dice. Bound by royal code and courtesy, Yudhishthira accepted, though his heart warned him of danger.

Shakuni rolled the dice with cunning skill, and one by one Yudhishthira lost his jewels, his chariots, his armies, and his kingdom. Still the game continued. In a moment of despair and confusion, he wagered his brothers, himself, and finally Draupadi. The court fell silent, then erupted into turmoil when Draupadi was summoned and humiliated before elders who should have defended dharma.

Bhishma, Drona, and Vidura looked on with sorrow and conflict. Vidura spoke against the injustice, but greed drowned wisdom. Draupadi questioned the court with fierce clarity: if Yudhishthira had already lost himself, by what right could he wager her? Her words shook the assembly, and for a brief moment truth stood brighter than power.

At last, under ominous signs and public shame, Dhritarashtra granted boons to Draupadi, restoring freedom to the Pandavas. Yet Duryodhana's envy returned, and a second game was played. The result was exile: thirteen years away from the throne, with the final year in concealment.

So the Pandavas entered the forest, not defeated in spirit but tempered by suffering. Their exile became a school of endurance, strategy, and inner strength. The injustice of the dice hall remained a wound in the heart of the kingdom, and from that wound the great war of Kurukshetra would one day arise.
